Matching Parents and Children with SQL in Python

SQL
Python
intergenerational mobility
Author

Clara Xu

Published

October 22, 2026

“Like father, like son” is an old saying, but how true is it for income? If your parents earned more than other parents, how much more will you earn as an adult? To answer this, we need to find each child’s parents in the data and follow both generations for many years. In this tutorial I show how to do this with SQL inside Python. If you know basic Python and SQL, you can follow along.

I use the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID), which has followed about 4,800 American families and their children since 1968. My data has 85,536 people, and for every year from 1968 to 2023 it tells us each person’s family ID, their relation to the household head, their age and their family’s income. The download steps and all code are on GitHub.

The raw file is very wide, with one row per person and one column for each variable in each year. SQL is much easier with a long table, so I first use pandas to turn it into person_years, with one row per person per year, and save it in SQLite. The whole match depends on two IDs. Your person ID never changes, like your name. Your family ID changes every year, like a house number on a new address list each year. If two people have the same family ID in the same year, they lived under the same roof that year. Here is a made-up example:

Made-up rows from the person-year table.
pid year family_id role age family income
1001 1970 452 head 41 9,800
1003 1970 452 child 12 9,800
1003 1995 2178 head 37 41,500

In 1970, person 1003 is a child in family 452, and person 1001 is the head of the same family, so 1001 is the parent. By 1995, person 1003 has grown up and leads a new family, so this row shows the child’s own adult income. Now we only need a few SQL steps:

  1. Build a family table. SELECT year and family ID, GROUP BY year, family_id, and use MAX() for family income and COUNT() for family size. This gives one row per family per year, 320,456 rows in total.
  2. Match children to parents. JOIN the table to itself ON the same year and the same family ID, and use WHERE to keep pairs where one person is a child and the other is the head, in 1968–1972.
  3. Keep clean matches. GROUP BY child and keep only children with HAVING COUNT(*) = 1. This drops children who lived with two different heads, for example after a divorce, and leaves 3,098 children.
  4. Build parent income. JOIN the parents to the family table, then use CASE WHEN inside MAX() and AVG() to get both their income in 1968 and their average income over 1968–1972.
  5. Build child income. JOIN the children to the family table WHERE the child is the head or spouse of their own family. This gives each child’s family income at every age from 25 to 50.

Step 2 is the heart of the tutorial:

SELECT c.pid AS child_id, p.pid AS parent_id, COUNT(*) AS years_together
FROM person_years AS c
JOIN person_years AS p
    ON c.year = p.year AND c.family_id = p.family_id
WHERE c.role = 'child' AND p.role = 'head'
    AND c.year BETWEEN 1968 AND 1972
GROUP BY c.pid, p.pid;

It may look strange to join a table to itself, but think of it as holding two copies of the same list side by side. In one copy we look for children (c), and in the other we look for heads (p), and we pair them when the year and the family ID match. A child can be paired with the same parent in up to five years, so GROUP BY keeps one row per pair, and COUNT(*) tells us how many years they lived together. One more small step: a dollar in 1970 is not the same as a dollar in 2020, so I divide each family’s income by the average PSID family income in that year. A value of 1.0 then always means an average family.

Now we can ask how much of the income gap between parents is passed on to their children. Economists call this the intergenerational elasticity, or IGE. It is the slope \(\beta\) in

\[\log(y_{child}) = \alpha + \beta \log(y_{parent}) + \varepsilon\]

An IGE of 0.5 means that if one family earns 10% more than another, their children will earn about 5% more. I also use a rank version, where parents and children are ranked from 0 to 100 within their own generation:

\[rank_{child} = \alpha + \beta_1 \, rank_{parent} + \varepsilon\]

The regression is the easy part. The tricky part is which income to use. The table starts with just one year for each generation, then swaps one side at a time for an average. Every row uses the same 1,352 children.

Estimates for the same 1,352 children. Robust standard errors are between 0.02 and 0.06.
Parent income Child income Log IGE Rank slope
1968 only Age 27 only 0.31 0.27
1968–1972 average Age 27 only 0.35 0.29
1968 only Ages 35–44 average 0.37 0.36
1968–1972 average Ages 35–44 average 0.48 0.39

The answer moves a lot, from 0.31 to 0.48. On the parent side, one year can be unusual. A father might lose his job for a few months or get a one-time bonus. These ups and downs have nothing to do with his child, but they blur the picture and pull the slope toward zero. Averaging five years smooths them out and raises the IGE from 0.37 to 0.48.

On the child side, the problem is age. Think about your classmates: at 27, most of you probably earn about the same, but ten years later the gaps will be much bigger. Figure 1 repeats the estimate at every age from 25 to 45.

The log IGE is only 0.33–0.39 at ages 25–27 and mostly 0.45–0.55 after 30. This is called life-cycle bias. Haider and Solon (2006) show that people whose income will grow the most often start low, so measuring too early makes the link between generations look weaker than it is. The log IGE also jumps around, because a few very low incomes can move it a lot. Ranks put these cases near zero, so the rank slope is smoother. It is also low at age 25 (0.23), but after 30 it stays between 0.30 and 0.37.

Takeaway

Matching parents and children takes only one self-join on year and family ID, but its real value is that it lets us follow both generations over many years. One year of income gives an IGE of 0.31, while averages over several years give 0.48, so a single snapshot makes families look more mobile than they are.

Data: Panel Study of Income Dynamics, public use dataset, 1968–2023, Survey Research Center, Institute for Social Research, University of Michigan. Sample: children of the household head in 1968–1972, born 1952–1968, from the original SRC sample. Income is total family money income divided by the average PSID family income of that year. Parent averages need at least 3 of the 1968–1972 waves, and child averages need at least 3 years at ages 35–44. No survey weights. Code: GitHub.
