Can One Year of Data Prove the Stock Market Goes Up?

simulation
bootstrap
hypothesis testing
finance
Author

Clara Xu

Published

November 3, 2026

Most people agree that the stock market goes up in the long run. Over the past ten years, the S&P 500 rose by about 0.06% on an average day. But on a normal day, the market moves by about 1%, which is 20 times larger than the average. So how can we show that the average return is really above zero? And how much data do we need?

I use daily S&P 500 data from FRED, from November 2016 to October 2026, which gives 2,510 daily returns. From these data, we know that the ten-year average is 0.058%. Then we pretend that we only see a small part of the data, and we check whether statistical methods can find the truth.

The first tool is the bootstrap. Suppose I only see 250 random days, which is about one year. I can compute an average, but if I had drawn a different set of 250 days, the average would be different. I do not know how far my average is from the true value. The bootstrap treats my sample as a copy of the population. It draws from the sample again and again with replacement and computes the average each time. After 5,000 rounds, the distribution of these averages shows how much my answer could change, and the middle 95% is the 95% confidence interval.

With one year of data, the interval runs from −0.14% to 0.12%. It still includes zero, so one year of data cannot tell us much yet. With more days, the interval becomes narrower, and our estimate becomes more precise.

What does a “95% confidence interval” really mean? If we draw samples many times and build an interval with the same method each time, about 95% of the intervals will contain the true value. To check this, I draw 100 different samples, build an interval from each one, and count how many of them contain the true average of 0.058%.

With large samples, this method works well. With 1,000 days, the 95% intervals contain the true value 98 times out of 100. With only 60 days, they contain it only 91 times. Short samples often miss the few days when the market moves a lot, so the market looks calmer than it really is. So the bootstrap works best when the sample is large enough to show the full range of the data.

Next is the hypothesis test. The null hypothesis says that the true average daily return is zero. Over the last year, the average was 0.045%. Could this be just luck? To test this, I build a world where the null hypothesis is true. I shift last year’s returns so that their average is exactly zero, and then I run the bootstrap 10,000 times.

These 10,000 averages form the distribution under the null hypothesis. The rejection region is the most extreme 5% at the two ends of this distribution. If the real average falls inside it, we reject the null hypothesis. The p-value is the share of the 10,000 averages that are at least as far from zero as last year’s real average. For the last year, the p-value is 0.38. This means that even if stocks had no real upward trend, a year as good as the last one would happen quite often. So we cannot reject the null hypothesis yet. The good news is that more data makes the picture much clearer.

The p-value falls from 0.38 with one year of data to 0.20 with two years and 0.13 with five years. With all ten years, it drops to 0.009, and we can finally reject the null hypothesis. Each extra year of data removes a little more luck from the average.

Takeaway

The stock market does go up on average, but daily returns are so noisy that one year, or even five years, of data cannot prove it. Only with ten years of data does the p-value fall below 0.05. The simulations show that a 95% confidence interval works as expected when the sample is large enough, and with more data, the distribution under the null hypothesis becomes narrower and the real trend appears.

Data: S&P 500 daily closing values from FRED (series SP500), November 2016 to October 2026. Returns are simple daily percent changes. All simulations use a fixed random seed. Code and data are available on GitHub.
